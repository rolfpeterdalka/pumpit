# Compressible mode (gas)

Pump It calculates networks of a gas as well as networks of a liquid. The user picks the **regime** of the model: incompressible (liquid, as before) or compressible (gas), and, for a gas, the **thermal model**: isothermal or adiabatic. The compressible mode follows the *compressible mode specification* (version 1, 19 October 2026, and its revision of 20 October 2026, which adds R-13: the pressures of the model are gauge), written for the earlier code base of the app; this file maps it onto Pump It and records the decisions that the specification leaves open or that the adaptation required (its §1 asks for this record).

Clean room (§1.1): only the specification, the code of this repository and public engineering knowledge (compressible flow in ducts, gas thermodynamics) were used. No other hydraulic program, manual, example file or installation folder was opened; for that reason `tests/validate-fth.js`, which reads example files of another program, was not run during this work.

## Decisions

**G-01 Module and namespace.** `src/core/gas/gas.js` holds appendix A with its statements unchanged (checked token by token, comments aside), except the names of the thermal models, in English like every stored value (G-04); its comments are in English, as the code base requires. It attaches itself to `PumpIt.gas` (one global namespace) and to `module.exports` when required from Node; `window.PumpItGas` is not created.

**G-02 Choosing the calculation.** `PumpIt.solver.run` hands a model whose `system.regime` is `'compressible'` to `PumpIt.gas.solveModelGas`, after removing the drawing fields, as it does for a liquid. The incompressible path is unchanged: every example and scenario gives byte-identical results in the three languages, against a record taken before the work.

**G-03 Tests.** `tests/gas.test.cjs` is appendix B (T1–T11: the checks of the appendix, with their names and messages in English) followed by T12 onwards for the models of the app; it loads the module of the repository, or a file given as its argument (the usage of the appendix). `tests/motor.test.cjs` runs `tests/run-tests.js`, the suite of the incompressible engine. `npm test` runs both.

**G-04 Values.** The fields take the values of §8, named in English like every value Pump It stores: `regime` `'incompressible'` | `'compressible'`, `gasMode` `'isothermal'` | `'adiabatic'`. A model saved with the Portuguese names of the specification (before 19 October 2026) still opens: `PumpIt.model.normalizeRegime` reads them as the English ones, in the system and in the scenarios.

**G-05 Both fluids kept.** `system.fluid` stays the liquid, as in every model saved before; `system.fluidGas` holds the gas. The regime chooses which one the calculation uses, so a change of regime moves nothing and scenario overrides keep their paths (R-03, §8.5). A gas written as the active fluid (`system.fluid.kind: 'gas'`, the layout of §8.1) becomes `fluidGas` on load, its numbers read in SI, and the liquid goes back to the default one.

**G-06 Gas data.** `fluidGas = { gas, name, MW, k, Z, mu, T }`: `gas` is a library id or `'custom'`; `mu` and `T` are quantities with the unit typed (cP, Pa·s…; °C, K…). A library gas takes MW and k from the library and μ from Sutherland's law at T; a custom gas gives MW, k and μ; Z is always the user's. Library: air, nitrogen, oxygen, methane, natural gas (specific gravity 0.6), carbon dioxide, hydrogen.

**G-07 Pressure at the nodes.** Junctions carry the static pressure in the isothermal model (R-31) and the stagnation pressure in the adiabatic one (R-32). Boundaries keep their nature: a reservoir is gas at rest (stagnation), an imposed pressure is static or stagnation as chosen in the junction (Pump It already had this choice), and an outside pressure (nozzle, exit valve) is static. A link converts at its ends: from a stagnation node the gas enters through an isentropic acceleration, P₁ = P₀·(1 + (k−1)/2·M₁²)^(−k/(k−1)); into a stagnation node its outlet stagnation pressure is what meets the node. At low speed this is the incompressible treatment of the boundaries (P6).

**G-08 Pipe relation.** Isothermal and horizontal: the closed form of §9.4 (R-31). Isothermal with a difference of elevation, and every adiabatic pipe: the march (R-21, R-23, R-32). K elements (valves, fittings, orifices), which have no length: the closed forms, isothermal with f·L/D + ΣK = K and Fanno with f·L*/D = K. The friction factor is Pump It's (Colebrook, 64/Re in laminar flow, 64·φ/Re in an annulus or a rectangle, the linear transition) with Re = G·D/μ, constant along a pipe since μ is constant. ΣK (fittings and the entrance and exit K of the junctions for the flow direction) is added to f·L/D in the closed form and distributed as f + ΣK·D/L in the march (§9.8). The laminar correction of the K values is the incompressible one.

**G-09 Network.** Newton-Raphson on the pressures of the free nodes until every mass balance closes (§10, R-30), with, as further unknowns, the flows of the links that impose a pressure, a pressure difference or a flow given as a volume (control valves, links without loss). The flow of every other link comes from the pressures of its two nodes: its relation is solved for the mass flux by a bracketed secant and capped at the critical flux when it chokes; its derivatives come from differences at the solution. Dense Gaussian elimination with equilibration (the incompressible one), line search, pressures kept positive, a small damping when the matrix is nearly singular. Converged when every free node balances within 1·10⁻⁸ of the flow through it (R-40). First pressures: the linear theory of gas networks on P², damped, 12 passes. `solveGasNetworkIso` stays in `gas.js` as the verified reference of the classic method (T10, T11); the app does not call it because it needs what that function does not have: f(Re), K per flow direction, elevation, choking, static and stagnation boundaries, elements and devices.

**G-10 Tiny drives.** Below 10⁻⁹ of the pressure, the flow of a link is linear in its drive, so it has a finite slope through zero where a K or a constant f gives a square root.

**G-11 March step.** 100 steps (§9.6); a step changes M by at most 0.005 and by at most 5 % of what is left to the limit, and a step with no subsonic solution is cut by four — the refinement of §9.7, applied at any Mach. Error against exact Fanno: about 10⁻⁶ in pressure when the outlet Mach is 0.4, 5·10⁻⁵ at 0.6, 2·10⁻⁴ at 0.77 and 10⁻³ at 0.94; critical flux within 1.5·10⁻⁵ (the uniform march of the appendix, 200 steps: 10⁻⁴).

**G-12 Choking.** A link chokes when no subsonic state gives the flux its nodes ask for: its flow is then the critical one (the largest flux that does not choke), the pressure downstream no longer changes it, and a critical warning is given (R-24). The limit is M = 1/√k in the isothermal model and M = 1 in the adiabatic one; the results show the state at the outlet of the pipe, before the expansion to the pressure of the node.

**G-13 Orifice.** A K element whose flow also cannot exceed the critical flow of its vena contracta, an isentropic nozzle of area Cc·Ao: Cc = Cd for the Cd model, 0.61 for a sharp edge, and 0.61 + 0.39·(1 − 10^(−7.7·r/dO)) for a rounded edge (an interpolation made up for Pump It after the edge term of the Idelchik formula it already uses). A restriction orifice therefore chokes at its hole.

**G-14 Valves.** A K element: the K given, or the K that the liquid relations of Cv and Kv give at the inlet area — the same, flow-free loss as in the incompressible mode. The critical pressure ratio of a valve trim is not modelled; the K element chokes at the area of the pipe.

**G-15 Normal conditions.** 0 °C and 101.325 kPa, ideal gas (Z = 1), for the normal density and normal volume flows.

**G-16 Junction types.** Reservoir: fixed stagnation pressure, the temperature of its gas. Imposed pressure: fixed static or stagnation pressure. Imposed flow and branch: a mass flow; a volume flow is read at the conditions of the node (§8.4). Valve, orifice and generic: K elements; the curve of a generic component is read against the volume flow at the inlet conditions. Control valve (PRV, PSV, FCV, PDCV): the states and rules of the incompressible device with the gas relations; the basis of a pressure set point (static or stagnation) is converted with the Mach number of the pipe at that end. Exit valve: a K element from the end of the pipe to the outside static pressure (without a loss, an isentropic exit in the adiabatic model). Nozzle: isentropic flow through Cd·A, choked below the critical pressure ratio; a K factor gives CdA = K·√(1000/2), as in the incompressible mode. Pump: off, a closed link or a link without loss; running, a compressor (phase 4). Pump It has no check valve, so the state loop of §10.4 for check valves has nothing to act on; the device loop serves the control valves.

**G-17 Hazen-Williams.** It has no meaning for a gas: such pipes are calculated with Darcy and the roughness stored in the pipe (0.046 mm if none), and a caution lists them (§8.2).

**G-18 Results.** The fields of R-25 beside the incompressible ones: Q is the volume flow at the start of the reference direction of the pipe and Qout at its end, as ρ, V, T and M (Q·ρ = ṁ at each end, both negative when the gas flows against the reference direction; revised in the review of 6c4f0fa, before it they followed the flow direction), and QN is the normal one; V is the larger of the two ends; T, T₀, ρ and M at both ends; heads (HGL, EGL, hL) are left out (NaN) for a gas. The drop of stagnation pressure is split between friction and the K values in the ratio of f·L/D to ΣK, an estimate, since the march does not separate them. `fluid.rho` of the results is the density at the mean fixed pressure, for the places of the interface that need one density.

**G-19 Warnings.** Choking (critical); Mach above `system.machLimit` (0.7 by default); velocity above `system.gasVelLimit` (30 m/s by default); and those the incompressible mode also gives.

**G-20 Gravity and reservoirs.** g = g₀ × `system.gravity`, as for a liquid. The "liquid level" of a reservoir is the elevation where its pressure applies; the column of gas from there to a pipe end is part of the pipe, as the column of liquid is.

**G-21 Speed of the tests.** The hot code (`flow.js`, `newton.js`) keeps local names for the Math functions: in the Node `vm` contexts of the tests a global lookup is about 16 times slower. Results do not change.

**G-22 Where the regime is chosen.** The "Fluid and system" dialog of the specification is the *System properties* window: the regime is its first section, two buttons, and the thermal model, the gas and the design limits follow it. The stamp of R-01 sits next to the model title ("Gas · isothermal · Air"); a click on it opens the window, as do *Edit → System properties…* and the button of the inspector when nothing is selected.

**G-23 Gas results in the interface.** The result tables have columns of their own for a gas (`GAS_COLS`); the inspector, the right-click card and the junction views show mass flows where a liquid shows volume flows, and add the Mach number, both pressures and the temperature. On the drawing, a gas adds mass flow and Mach number and drops the hydraulic grade line; its velocity is coloured over the range of the model, since the fixed scale is a water scale. The profile chart draws the points of the march (or of the closed form) inside each pipe. The pump × system chart is not drawn for a gas.

**G-24 The advice of §5.** In the incompressible mode, a fluid lighter than 50 kg/m³ (as light as a gas) in a pipe past Mach 0.3 — speed of sound √(k·P/ρ), with the k of the gas of the model — or with a drop above 10% of its absolute pressure brings a caution that suggests the compressible mode; liquids are not affected. In the compressible mode, when every pipe stays below Mach 0.1, every drop (pipes, valves, fittings, discharges) stays below 2% of the absolute pressure and no compressor runs, an *information* says that the incompressible mode would give practically the same. Information is a new, fourth level of message, not counted in the badge of the Results tab.

**G-25 Normal volume flows.** A unit category of its own (`normFlow`: Nm³/s, Nm³/min, Nm³/h, Nm³/d), part of the flow inputs; a mass flow is ρ_N·Q_N (G-15). A liquid given a normal unit uses its own density.

**G-26 From a library gas to a custom one.** Choosing *Custom gas* copies the molar mass, k, the viscosity at the given temperature and the name of the library gas chosen before, in one step (one undo).

**G-27 Compressor and blower (§10.5).** A running pump of a gas model is a compressor whose flow is an unknown with a row of its own: a fixed ratio of stagnation pressures; or the curve, its head read as the isentropic head of the gas against the volume flow at the suction, r = [1 + g·H·(k−1)/(k·Z·R·T₀)]^(k/(k−1)), which is ΔP = ρgH for a small head, the pump of a liquid; or a fixed flow (mass, volume at the suction, normal volume); or the speed control of the discharge or suction static pressure, with the speed found by bisection as for a pump. The polytropic efficiency (the efficiency curve, or a field of its own, 75% by default) gives T₀ out = T₀ in·r^((k−1)/(k·η)) and the power to the gas ṁ·c_p·ΔT₀. In the adiabatic model that temperature goes into the network (the temperature loop); in the isothermal model an aftercooler is assumed and the discharge temperature is only reported. A fixed ratio exists only in the compressible mode (the checklist refuses it for a liquid); a pump that is off is a closed link or a tie. For the first pressures, the ratio (or the ratio of the head at 60% of the curve) enters the linear theory (G-30).

**G-28 Temperatures of the adiabatic model (R-12, R-32).** The temperature given with the gas is the stagnation temperature of the sources. A reservoir, an imposed pressure, an entering imposed flow and the source of a node have an optional field of their own (`gasT`), shown in the adiabatic model; empty means the temperature of the sources. The adiabatic calculation starts from the isothermal solution of the same network (§10.3): on a grid of 276 pipes with elevations the adiabatic Newton then needs 4 iterations instead of 10, although the isothermal solution costs about as much there (both use the march).

**G-29 Choking of junctions.** A choked pipe is critical (R-24). A choked valve, fitting, orifice or nozzle is a *warning*: a restriction orifice and a blow-off nozzle choke by design.

**G-30 Linear theory with relations.** The first pressures come from the linear theory on u = P², extended with the links that impose a relation linear in u: a ratio (u_b = r²·u_a), a set pressure (u = set²) and a tie or a set difference; their flows are unknowns of that linear system too. Without it, a compressor or a reducing valve started the network at the inlet pressure, and a plant with fixed consumers fell into choking before the Newton could recover.

**G-31 Flows set as a volume.** A flow control valve or a compressor whose flow is given as a volume at the inlet conditions is a fixed flow (for the connectivity analysis) with a row of its own, ṁ = Q·ρ(inlet).

**G-32 Speed (R-42).** Measured in Node with the core in the main context, as a browser runs it: 276 pipes with elevations, 0.25 s isothermal and 0.4 s adiabatic; the same grid horizontal, 0.04 s isothermal and 0.17 s adiabatic; 566 pipes with elevations, 0.6 to 0.9 s. The calculation is synchronous, like the incompressible one: the app opens straight from disk, where browsers do not allow the web workers that would move it off the interface thread. Models of tens of pipes take a few milliseconds.

**G-33 Help.** Two chapters in the help window — *Gas (compressible mode)*, for the user, and *Technical reference for gases*, with the equations — and a step of the guided tour on the stamp. `tests/check-i18n.js` now also checks every reference inside the help (labels, links, formulas, examples, areas of the screen, tour steps) in every language. The help texts that said the app calculates only liquids were updated.

**G-34 Hydraulic model of the liquid.** The incompressible model was reviewed while the gas path was built (laminar correction of the K values, end losses, static boundaries, Cv and Kv, nozzles, devices, results); no inconsistency was found that needed a correction. The refactors the gas path needed — the pipe data in SI shared with the gas (`PumpIt.solver.pipeData`), the effective area of a nozzle, the K of an orifice and the state of a control valve as helpers, the links that tie two pressures in the connectivity analysis — keep every result of the liquid byte-identical.

**G-35 Gauge and absolute pressures (R-13, revision of 20 October 2026).** The revision makes the pressures of the model gauge, relative to `system.patm`, as for a liquid, while the gas is calculated in absolute pressure. Pump It already stores each pressure with its unit, gauge (g) or absolute (a), and converts it to absolute SI on input with the atmospheric pressure of the model, so the `P = p + patm` of R-13 holds for any unit and the calculation did not change. The results keep absolute pressures in SI, with `patm`; the interface shows gauge pressures by default (kgf/cm²(g), the default output unit of the app, in place of the kPa of the specification), and the unit selector of every pressure column, panel and card offers the absolute ones: the two forms of R-13. What changed:
- The example of appendix D was built with the absolute pressures the first version asked for (5, 2.5 and 2 bar(a)); it now has the gauge ones of the revision, 5, 2.5 and 2 bar(g). The drops are 42% and 50% of the absolute inlet pressure, and the flow from the vessel goes from 1.558 to 1.770 kg/s. No other example changed.
- In the compressible mode, a pressure the gas calculation takes must be above vacuum once a gauge value is added to the atmospheric pressure: the surface pressure of a reservoir, an imposed pressure, the outlet pressure of an exit valve, the external pressure of a nozzle, the set pressure of a reducing or sustaining valve and the discharge or suction set pressure of a compressor. Otherwise the field stays pending in the checklist, by name (the "data error with a clear message" of R-13); before, such a value reached the calculation and gave a meaningless result, such as a choked pipe flowing into a vessel at −1.5 bar(g). An atmospheric pressure that is not above zero is pending too. A gauge pressure below zero that stays above vacuum (a vacuum line) is accepted, and a liquid is not checked.
- The help (chapter *Gas*) says how pressures are typed and shown; `tests/gas.test.cjs` compares the example in absolute pressures (T13) and checks R-13 (T29).

**G-36 Models of .aro files.** A `.aro` file of a gas network opens as a model of the compressible mode ([aro-import.md](aro-import.md)). The fields its format description asked for were already in Pump It: a unit on every flow (mass, local volume or normal volume), the temperature of each source (G-28), a fixed f, and the standard conditions, used during the import. So the model and the calculation did not change. A compressor curve of pressure rise against flow becomes the head curve of G-27 at a suction state, the one stored in the file when it has results (A-21).

**G-37 Links of almost no loss (time simulation, 2 November 2026).** The finite gas vessels of the time simulation ([time-simulation.md](time-simulation.md), T-30 and T-31) join a vessel to a short, wide pipe, whose loss is tiny against its pressures. Two changes let the solver handle such a link, and the cases of `tests/gas.test.cjs` keep their results:

- `src/core/gas/newton.js`: a node joined by such a link may not balance to 10⁻⁸ of its flow, since a few units in the last place of its pressure move its flows by more than that. When the Newton step falls below 32 units in the last place of every unknown with every misfit under 10⁻⁶, nothing can improve the balance, and the solution is taken as converged.
- `src/core/gas/flow.js`: the slopes of a link come from finite differences of its march, which round to nothing when its drive is of the size of the rounding. When the drive is below 10⁻⁶ of the upstream pressure, or the differences give no usable slope, the link takes the slopes of a quadratic loss, ∂G/∂P_up = G/(2·ΔP) and ∂G/∂P_down = −G/(2·ΔP).

Before them the adiabatic discharge of case T19 of the time simulation diverged, to a flow of −2017 kg/s, and the isothermal one of T18 did not converge.

**G-38 Pipes of almost no friction on a slope (review of ab3e0d1, 25 November 2026).** A pipe without friction and without K values tied the pressures of its ends, so a frictionless rise of 500 m delivered the gas at the pressure it left with. A test of continuity as the friction tends to zero showed more: of 42 cases (both thermal models, level, rise and descent, f from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁹ and zero), 28 failed, were wrong or did not converge. The linear theory ignored the column of gas, so the first pressures were far enough to choke the pipe; on a descent of low friction the gas slows down and gains more pressure than the friction takes, so the pressures of the ends no longer fix the flow; and between nodes of two kinds (G-07) a loss below one velocity head has no solution (T-38 of [time-simulation.md](time-simulation.md)). What changed:

- `src/core/gas/network.js`: a pipe whose smallest loss, f·L/D for a given factor (0 without friction, no bound for the Darcy factor) plus the smaller of its K values, is not above 4·g·|Δz|/(Z·R·T), plus 1 between nodes of two kinds (one of them free since G-42), takes its flow as an unknown, with the pressure at its end b as its row. Without friction the result is the column of gas at low flow, P₂/P₁ = exp(−g·Δz/(Z·R·T)) in the isothermal model (the isentropic column in the adiabatic one, G-39); with a little friction it is the same march, so the pressure is continuous as f tends to zero. Only the elements of the junctions without loss (a valve of K 0, a pump stopped open) still tie two pressures, and so does a pipe of no length.
- `src/core/gas/flow.js`: `pipeOut` marches such a pipe from its end a whatever the direction of the flow, the losses with the sign of the flow, the gas at the T₀ of the node it comes from (against the flow, plus what it gains by falling to end a, G-39); with no flow there is no loss.
- `src/core/gas/solver.js`: the linear theory takes the column of gas of every pipe on a slope, u_b = r²·u_a with r = exp(−g·Δz/(Z·R·T)).
- `src/core/gas/newton.js` and `results.js`: the state of the march gives the Mach at the ends, which a compressor at a fixed ratio needs to convert to stagnation pressure (0.04 % off without it), and the values and the profile of the results.

All 42 cases solve; at low flow the ratio of pressures matches the column to 2·10⁻⁹. A short pipe of a given factor next to a reservoir changes formulation, with results that change only in the last places (3.5·10⁻¹²). A pipe of Darcy friction keeps the old formulation, so a short, wide one between nodes of two kinds keeps the limit (1) of T-38.

**G-39 Potential energy in the adiabatic model (review of 6043c80, 25 November 2026).** The energy balance of the march had no term for the elevation: a frictionless pipe of air rising 1000 m without heat or work delivered the gas at the stagnation temperature it entered with, 300 K, where the balance c_p·(T₀,out − T₀,in) + g·Δz = 0 asks for 290.24 K (309.76 K on the descent). The energy residual of a step is now c_p·(T′ − T) + (V′² − V²)/2 + g·dz_step = 0, so c_p·T₀ + g·z is kept along an adiabatic pipe whatever its friction. The reference march of appendix A (`gas.js`) has the same gap and is kept as the appendix states it; tests T1 to T11 use it on level pipes only. What changed:

- `src/core/gas/flow.js`: the residual of `march`, used by every adiabatic pipe; and `pipeOut`, which marches from end a whatever the direction (G-38): against the flow the gas reaches end a with T₀ of node b plus g·Δz/c_p, and the band of tiny flows blends the two ends from that value.
- `src/core/gas/solver.js`: `mixTemperatures` carries into the next node the stagnation temperature that leaves the pipe, T₀ − g·Δz/c_p along the flow, so a compressor, a valve and a vessel of the time simulation downstream get it too.

The gas column at rest keeps its convention, isothermal at the temperature of the node, so a dead end gives the pressure it gave before. At low flow the march of the adiabatic model tends to the isentropic column, P₂/P₁ = (1 − g·Δz/(c_p·T))^(c_p/(Z·R)); between the two columns lies a narrow band where the flow is practically zero, about 3.5·(g·Δz/(c_p·T))² of the pressure (13 Pa for 100 m at 3.5 bar). The isothermal model does not change: its static temperature is imposed. In the report case the outlet pressures move from 313.49 to 312.90 kPa(a) on the rise and from 393.68 to 392.97 kPa(a) on the descent, since the cooler gas is denser; in the compressor example of the review of 6c4f0fa the gas falls 3 m to the suction, and its discharge temperature goes from 293.5643 to 293.5936 K.

**G-40 Results that depend on the direction or the end (review of 319654f, 25 November 2026).** Two results of a gas read one side only. The equivalent Cv of a valve or a control valve took the area of the pipe drawn at its inlet, whereas its K is referred to the pipe the flow comes from: with 100 and 150 mm pipes and the flow against the drawing, the same valve and flow gave a Cv of 425.0 instead of 188.9, and an event on the Cv fired in one drawing only. The Cv now takes the area of the pipe the flow comes from. The extremes of the time simulation took the volume flow and the velocity at the inlet of a pipe only, whereas a gas expands along it (918 m³/h in and 1204 m³/h out in pipe 1 of the air example); the extremes of a gas pipe now cover both ends and tell the end, as those of its pressures do. A liquid has one flow and one velocity per pipe and is not changed. The exit valve had the same fault (review of a952425, item 2): its equivalent K, and the Cv from it, took the density of the end "out" of its pipe and the larger velocity of the two ends, two different sections when the pipe was drawn from the valve. In the air example with J3 as an exit valve of K 5, the same discharge of 0.81473 kg/s gave a Cv of 129.77 drawn towards the valve and 145.25 drawn from it, and an event at a Cv of 140 fired in the second drawing only. The K and the Cv now take the density and the velocity of the end of the pipe at the valve, found by the id of the junction, in both drawings; an inflow, which warns, takes the same end.

**G-41 Static imposed pressure and the K of its pipe (review of a952425, item 9, 26 November 2026).** A static imposed pressure is the static pressure at the end of its single pipe, as the help says and the results of the pipe show it. In the incompressible mode the node held that pressure beyond the K values of the end, while the row of the pipe showed the section before them: with K in = 4, a static pressure of 100 kPa(a) showed 166.67 kPa(a) at the end of the pipe, and the flow was 163.24 m³/h instead of 282.74. In both modes, and in the water hammer, the K values of the end of the pipe at a static imposed pressure are now left out (`pipeEndK` of `src/core/junctions/shared.js`, `atPipeEnd` of the imposed pressure), with a caution when the model holds one, and the pressure holds at the end of the pipe. A stagnation pressure keeps its K values, beyond which it holds.

**G-42 Short pipes between nodes of two kinds (follow-up of the review of a952425, 26 November 2026).** Since G-38 a pipe with a given friction factor or without friction, whose losses may fall below one velocity head between nodes of two kinds (G-07), takes its flow as an unknown, with the pressure at its end b as its row. That formulation failed in three ways. Between two fixed pressures its first flow was zero, where the row is flat in the flow, and the system was singular: a reservoir at 1.05 bar(a) feeding a static imposed pressure of 1 bar(a) through a pipe of 100 mm with f = 0.02 failed for every f·L/D up to 1, in both thermal models. With a free end the linear theory took the pipe as a tie of its two pressures, which gave too large a first flow: in the isothermal model, where the nodes of a valve are static, a valve of K 2 between reservoirs at 2 and 1.5 bar(a), with pipes of f·L/D 0.8 to 1 on both sides, diverged. And the march has no value beyond the critical flow, so such a pipe could not choke: the same valve from 3 to 1 bar(a), whose outlet pipe chokes, failed up to f·L/D 1. What changed:

- `src/core/gas/network.js`: the velocity head counts in the bound of G-38 only when an end of the pipe is a free node. Between two fixed pressures of two kinds nothing else sets the flow, and the pipe is an ordinary one: the flow goes from the higher pressure to the lower, as for a liquid, and may choke. A static pressure that feeds a reservoir through a pipe of f·L/D below 1 with an exit K of 0 gives the critical flow, since the losses stay below the velocity head of the static end.
- `src/core/gas/solver.js`: the linear theory takes such a pipe as a loss of F + 1 velocity heads, the velocity head of its static end included. When the guess has no flow for it, or a zero one, its first flow follows from the pressures of its ends through the same relation, in the direction they give; this covers the adiabatic model, which starts from the pressures of the isothermal solution without its flows. When the Newton method fails or does not converge after a pipe whose flow is an unknown choked on the way, that pipe becomes an ordinary one, its flow following its pressures up to the critical flow, and the same states are solved again.
- `src/core/gas/newton.js`: a first flow beyond the critical flow of its pipe leaves the march without a value; the first flows of such pipes are then halved until it has one, at most 40 times. The method reports the pipes that choked during its iterations.

The three cases solve in both thermal models and in both drawings, and the flow is continuous across f·L/D = 1. In the isothermal model, from 1.05 bar(a): 0.60232 kg/s at f·L/D 1 and 0.60085 kg/s at 1.01; through the valve: 1.66497 and 1.66143 kg/s; from 3 bar(a), the outlet pipe choked: 2.87155 and 2.86741 kg/s. At a drop of 50 Pa the flow matches the incompressible ΔP = (1 + F)·ρV²/2 within 10⁻⁴. The two drawings give the same flow within 3.4·10⁻⁸, the difference between a march from one end and from the other. Each step of the time simulation gives the steady state of its pressures. A pipe of Darcy friction keeps the ordinary formulation, and with it the limit (1) of T-38 of [time-simulation.md](time-simulation.md) for a short, wide one. The examples and the earlier checks keep their results.

**G-43 Control valves that cannot control (follow-up of the review of a952425, 26 November 2026).** As with a liquid, a control valve starts controlling, and the review after a solution opens or closes it when it cannot control. With a gas the network may have no solution at all while the valve controls, so the review never came: between a supply and a reservoir at 1.5 bar(a), with pipes of 20 m, a PRV of 2.5 bar(a) fed at 2 bar(a), and a PSV of 2.5 bar(a) fed at 2 bar(a), gave a singular system in both thermal models, and an FCV of 3 kg/s fed at 2 bar(a) a result not converged with 1060 kg/s in its inlet pipe. A PSV fed at 5 bar(a), opened by the review, did not converge from the pressures it held controlling. What changed:

- `src/core/junctions/control-valve.js`: the device of the compressible mode tells the states a controlling valve may take when the network has no solution with it controlling, in order: a PRV fully open, then closed; a PSV closed, then fully open; an FCV its failure action. A PDCV never fails (I-61), and a special condition and "always control" keep the state.
- `src/core/gas/solver.js`: when a solution fails or does not converge, a pass that started from the solution of other states is solved again from the first pressures. If it still fails, every valve that may leave its control takes its first state, and the review goes on from there; when the review sends a valve back to control, where there was no solution, it takes its next state. When that fails too, the first failure is the result, as before. A valve of "always control" in a network without a solution is solved once without the option: if it then does not control, the calculation stops with a message that names it and the state it would take (`core.gas.cannotControl`).

The cases above give a PRV fully open at 2 bar(a) and closed at 1 bar(a), a PSV closed at 1 and 2 bar(a) and fully open at 5 bar(a), and an FCV fully open or closed as its failure action, each with the flow of the valve held in that state by its special condition, in both thermal models. The time simulation, where "always control" is off (T-15 of [time-simulation.md](time-simulation.md)), gives at each step the steady state. A network with such a valve still spends the iterations of the attempt without a solution first. The examples and the earlier checks keep their results.
